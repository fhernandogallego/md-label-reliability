Raw trajectories of the \nLong{} long runs were read as a stream, keeping only ion positions
(lithium and the nitrogen of TFSI), the polymer atoms of the types that the platform lists as
solvating, and the system centre of mass. That list of types is the same for every system while
the type numbering is not, so the kept polymer atoms are not always those that coordinate lithium
(often they are hydrogen or carbon atoms); they are used only to follow the mobility of the
polymer. The origin-averaged MSD computed from
them reproduces the curves distributed by the platform to within a relative deviation of
\num{\valMaxDev} in every run, for lags up to 80\% of the run. Without subtracting the
centre-of-mass motion, the MSD at a lag of \SI{25}{\nano\second} would be inflated by a median
factor of \driftMed{} and by up to a factor of \driftMax{} (more than 10\% in \driftFracPct\% of
the runs); the largest factors occur in the slowest systems, where a drift of the whole system
by a few nanometres is comparable to the displacement of the lithium ions.

\section{Slowdown within the long runs}\label{si:sec:slowdown}
Each long run is split into ten consecutive \SI{5}{\nano\second} segments, and the first segment
is compared with the five late ones (25 to \SI{50}{\nano\second}). Mobilities are end-point
estimates over each segment, after subtracting the centre-of-mass motion, for lithium, for the
nitrogen of TFSI and for the polymer atoms kept from the raw trajectory (\slowMatN{} systems that
have any). Production is at constant volume (NVT); the potential energy and pressure are the
segment means of the \SI{100}{\pico\second} averages written by the simulations. Ion clustering
uses the lithium-O(TFSI) clusters computed in every frame during the simulations: the size of
the cluster that contains each lithium, and the fraction of lithium ions whose cluster contains no
anion oxygen; the number of anion nitrogens around each lithium uses a cutoff at the first
minimum of the lithium-nitrogen radial distribution function of each system.

Two runs of the same system differ by more than a factor of 1.5 in \RepEndDiffPct\% of the
replicated systems. The replicated subset is not range-restricted (spread of $\ln\Dapp$
\RepEndSdSub{} against \RepEndSdAll{} in the whole screening set). The effective exponent of an
individual \SI{5}{\nano\second} run is not reproducible (noise \RepBetaSd, rank correlation
between runs $\RepBetaRho$): it characterises the population, not a single system.

\paragraph{Structural and dynamic noise.} The dynamic noise is estimated from the \techoEndDynN{}
screening pairs (the \SI{5}{\nano\second} run against the first \SI{5}{\nano\second} of the long run
from the same structure) and the total from the \nRep{} replicated systems. The short run of a pair
is the original replica of the same system, so the two differences are correlated (correlation
\techoEndJointDiffCorr{} on the \techoEndJointN{} systems that have both). Resampling those systems
jointly, the structure accounts for \techoEndJointFracStructPct\% of the run-to-run variance of the
end-point label (\techoEndJointFracStructLo{} to \techoEndJointFracStructHi\%; separate resampling
of the two sets, as in the main text: \techoEndFracStructPct\%, \techoEndFracStructLo{} to
\techoEndFracStructHi\%) and \techoAvgJointFracStructPct\% (\techoAvgJointFracStructLo{} to
\techoAvgJointFracStructHi\%) of that of the origin-averaged label.

\paragraph{Batches.} In the data set the original screening runs, the replicas and the long runs
belong to three different batches. A batch effect would appear as a shift between them or as a
different spread across systems. Neither is seen for the end-point label: the mean ratio original
to replica is \techoEndBatchRepRatio{} (95\% interval \techoEndBatchRepRatioLo{} to
\techoEndBatchRepRatioHi, $p=\techoEndBatchRepP$), the mean ratio of the short run to the first
\SI{5}{\nano\second} of its long run is \techoEndBatchPrefRatio{} (\techoEndBatchPrefRatioLo{} to
\techoEndBatchPrefRatioHi, $p=\techoEndBatchPrefP$), and the spread of $\ln\Dapp$ across systems is
\techoEndBatchSdOrig{} for the originals and \techoEndBatchSdRep{} for their replicas (Levene
$p=\techoEndBatchLeveneP$; \techoEndBatchSdOther{} in the rest of the screening set). The noise
estimates use the standard deviation of the differences, which excludes a constant offset; without
removing it the reliability is \techoEndRelUncentred{}, unchanged. Batch and initial structure
cannot be fully separated, because every replica is both new and from a later batch.

\section{End-point estimator}\label{si:sec:endpoint}
The end-point value of the \SI{5}{\nano\second} runs deviates from the power law fitted over
20\% to 60\% of the same curve with a standard deviation of \epSd{} in log units, a typical factor of
\epFactor{} (\cref{si:fig:endpoint}); \epOutOnePointFivePct\% of the systems deviate by more than
a factor of 1.5, and the rank correlation between the end-point estimate and the origin-averaged
estimate at half the run length is \epRho. This deviation combines the single-origin noise of the
end point with any departure of the curve from a power law; the replicated runs
(\cref{si:tab:replicas}) give the direct comparison of the two estimators.

\section{Adaptive allocation: algorithm and robustness}\label{si:sec:alloc}
\paragraph{Algorithm.} All quantities are in $\ln D$ of the end-point label. A campaign has
$N=\alN$ candidates, a target of the top $k$ and a mean budget of $b$ runs per candidate, each run
starting from a new, equilibrated structure.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \emph{Model.} System values $\mu_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{sys}}^2)$ with
        $\sigma_{\mathrm{sys}}=\alSdSys$; a run of system $i$ is $\mu_i+\varepsilon$ with
        $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma(\mu_i)^2)$ and
        $\sigma(\mu)=\max\{a+\gamma\,\mu/\sigma_{\mathrm{sys}},\,0.08\}$, $a=\alNoiseA$,
        $\gamma=\alNoiseB$, fitted so that the mean noise of the slower and faster halves matches the
        replicated systems.
  \item \emph{Posterior.} After $n_i$ runs with mean $\bar y_i$, the posterior of $\mu_i$ is
        approximated by a normal with precision $1/\sigma_{\mathrm{sys}}^2+n_i/\sigma(\bar y_i)^2$
        and mean $(n_i\bar y_i/\sigma(\bar y_i)^2)$ divided by that precision. Because the noise
        depends on $\mu_i$, the exact posterior is not normal; the approximation evaluates the noise
        at the observed mean (plug-in). Allocating instead with a constant-noise model, whose posterior
        is exactly normal, gives practically the same recall while the runs keep their
        mobility-dependent noise (see below), so the approximation does not drive the results.
  \item \emph{First round.} One run for every candidate.
  \item \emph{Batches.} While budget remains: take the cut-off $c_k$ as the $k$-th largest
        posterior mean, compute $p_i=\Phi[(m_i-c_k)/s_i]$ from the posterior mean $m_i$ and standard
        deviation $s_i$, and give one more run to the \alBatchPct\% of candidates with the largest
        $p_i(1-p_i)$.
  \item \emph{Selection.} The top $k$ by posterior mean. The metric is the fraction of the true
        top $k$, the $k$ largest $\mu_i$, that is selected.
\end{enumerate}
The true top set is therefore defined by the expected \SI{5}{\nano\second} label of each system,
not by its long-time diffusivity. Uniform replicates, successive halving and the two-stage
re-screening use the same model, posterior and selection; only the choice of who receives each
run differs. All random draws use fixed seeds, given in the code.

Underestimating the reliability makes the posterior trust each run less and spreads the
replicates more widely; overestimating it concentrates them. Neither changes the saving much
(\cref{si:tab:alloc-checks}). If instead the true reliability, not only the planned one, lies at
either end of its interval (keeping the total spread of the short runs, and with the allocation
using the correct value), the saving against four uniform replicates is
$\times$\alTrueLoSavingTopOne{} ($r=\alRelPlanLo$) and $\times$\alTrueHiSavingTopOne{}
($r=\alRelPlanHi$) for the top 1\%, $\times$\alTrueLoSavingTopFive{} and
$\times$\alTrueHiSavingTopFive{} for the top 5\%, and $\times$\alTrueLoSavingTopTen{} and
$\times$\alTrueHiSavingTopTen{} for the top 10\%: noisier labels reduce the saving but do not
remove it. The two-stage re-screening is as good as boundary-focused allocation
for the top 1\%, where the best shortlist is $8k$, but falls behind for broader targets, and a
shortlist that is too short cannot recover candidates that the first run ranked low.
